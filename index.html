02 / The concept

Translanguaging ≠ “just switching languages.”

The distinction matters because the teacher's intention changes the role language plays in learning.

Odilon Redon, The Tell-Tale Heart, 1883 — public-domain artwork
Code-switching
Language alternation.

Moving between named languages during communication. It may be spontaneous, situational, or interactional.

Lens: “Which language am I using now?”

Translanguaging
Meaning-making through the whole linguistic repertoire.

Multilingual speakers flexibly draw on their linguistic resources to understand, communicate, reason, and learn.

Lens: “What linguistic resources help me make meaning?”

García and Wei (2014) describe translanguaging as multilingual speakers using their languages as an integrated communication system rather than as isolated systems.
03 / Why it matters

Language can become a bridge to rigorous thinking.

Translanguaging can make complex content more accessible while preserving meaningful learning goals.

🧠

Deepens comprehension

💬

🌏

Fits Philippine classrooms

06 / Myth-busting

Let's retire five misconceptions.

“English only means better English.”

Not necessarily. Access to prior knowledge can support stronger target-language performance.

“It means students are lazy.”

Strategic linguistic flexibility can support cognitive access while maintaining rigorous outcomes.

“It is just code-switching.”

The pedagogical lens focuses on the learner's full repertoire for meaning-making.

“Teachers must speak every language.”

Peer collaboration and multilingual resources can distribute linguistic expertise.

“It lowers standards.” A scaffold changes the route to learning—not necessarily the learning target.

06 / Deep dive

From theory to classroom decision-making.

This section gives facilitators enough substance to move beyond a definition and examine what translanguaging actually looks like before, during, and after instruction.

Core principle

Students do not leave their linguistic resources at the classroom door.

In a multilingual classroom, learners may use Filipino, English, a regional language, or combinations of these resources while thinking, interpreting, explaining, negotiating meaning, and constructing knowledge.

Pedagogical implication: the teacher can intentionally design moments where those resources support access to the curriculum without abandoning the target learning outcome.

Important distinction

Translanguaging is not “allowing anything.”

Intentional translanguaging still requires planning. The teacher decides when linguistic flexibility supports learning, why it is being used, and what students must ultimately demonstrate.

Think: “What is the language doing for learning at this moment?”
The teacher's planning lens

Ask four questions before opening the language space.

01

What is the objective?

Identify the actual knowledge or skill students need to demonstrate. Do not let language choice obscure the intended learning outcome.

02

Where is the barrier?

Is the difficulty conceptual, linguistic, textual, procedural, or a combination? Translanguaging should respond to a real learning need.

03

Where can flexibility help?

Choose the stage where students may brainstorm, discuss, annotate, clarify, compare, rehearse, or explain ideas using their linguistic resources.

04

What remains accountable?

Specify the final product, evidence, performance, or criterion. The scaffold can be multilingual while the required demonstration remains aligned with the objective.

Misconceptions

Let's retire these five myths.

MYTH 01 — “English only means better English.”

Reframe: access to prior knowledge and conceptual understanding can support stronger target-language performance. The question is whether students ultimately meet the intended English-learning outcome.

MYTH 02 — “Translanguaging means students are lazy.”

Reframe: strategic use of familiar linguistic resources can increase cognitive access. The teacher still maintains rigorous thinking, evidence, and assessment criteria.

MYTH 03 — “It is just code-switching.”

Reframe: the pedagogical lens is different. Translanguaging focuses on how multilingual learners mobilize their repertoire for meaning-making and learning.

MYTH 04 — “The teacher has to speak every language.”

Reframe: teachers can facilitate peer collaboration, multilingual resources, glossaries, annotation, and learner expertise without being fluent in every language represented.

MYTH 05 — “It lowers standards.”

Reframe: a scaffold changes the route to learning, not necessarily the learning target. Rigorous success criteria remain visible.

09 / Full sample lesson

The Tell-Tale Heart: a complete translanguaging lesson application.

Designed to demonstrate that translanguaging can coexist with rigorous literary analysis, textual evidence, and English output.

Learning objective

Students will analyze how the narrator's thoughts, motivations, and behavior reveal an unreliable mind.

Success criteria:

  • Identify at least two textual details that reveal the narrator's mental state.
  • Explain how each detail supports an interpretation.
  • Construct an analytical claim in English.
  • Use appropriate textual evidence and reasoning.

Translanguaging purpose: provide linguistic access during interpretation and rehearsal while preserving the rigor of the final literary analysis.

Before reading

Activate the linguistic repertoire

Ask: “How do we describe a person who is nervous, obsessed, suspicious, or mentally disturbed?”

Students create a multilingual word bank. They may add Filipino or regional-language terms, English equivalents, examples, and connotations.

Teacher move: “Which word carries the strongest meaning? Does the English equivalent have exactly the same emotional force?”
During reading

Evidence hunt + bilingual reasoning

Students annotate selected passages. They may explain the narrator's behavior to a partner in their strongest language.

Prompt sequence:

  1. What does the narrator say?
  2. What does the narrator do?
  3. What does this reveal about the narrator's thinking?
  4. Which English phrase in the text is your strongest evidence?
After reading

Bridge into English analysis

Students complete a sentence frame:

“The narrator appears unreliable because ________. This is shown when he says, ‘________.’ This suggests ________.”

The multilingual discussion has done the cognitive preparation. The English response demonstrates the intended learning outcome.

Assessment

Do not assess the scaffold as though it were the target.

25%

Interpretation

Quality and plausibility of the literary interpretation.

25%

Evidence

Relevant and accurately integrated textual evidence.

25%

Reasoning

Clear explanation connecting evidence to the claim.

25%

English expression

Clarity and appropriateness of the final English response.
