02 / The concept

Translanguaging ≠ “just switching languages.”

The distinction matters because the teacher's intention changes the role language plays in learning.

Odilon Redon, The Tell-Tale Heart, 1883 — public-domain artwork
Code-switching
Language alternation.

Moving between named languages during communication. It may be spontaneous, situational, or interactional.

Lens: “Which language am I using now?”

Translanguaging
Meaning-making through the whole linguistic repertoire.

Multilingual speakers flexibly draw on their linguistic resources to understand, communicate, reason, and learn.

Lens: “What linguistic resources help me make meaning?”

García and Wei (2014) describe translanguaging as multilingual speakers using their languages as an integrated communication system rather than as isolated systems.
03 / Why it matters

Language can become a bridge to rigorous thinking.

Translanguaging can make complex content more accessible while preserving meaningful learning goals.

🧠

Deepens comprehension

Students can process difficult concepts through familiar language before consolidating understanding in English.

💬

Expands participation

More learners gain an entry point into discussion and reasoning.

🧩

Activates prior knowledge

Existing linguistic and cultural knowledge becomes a learning asset.

🤝

Supports collaboration

Peers can negotiate meaning and co-construct understanding.

✍️

Scaffolds academic English

Familiar-language thinking can bridge toward target-language output.

🌏

Fits Philippine classrooms

It recognizes multilingualism as part of learners' lived classroom reality.

04 / Teacher planning lens

Intentional—not random.

Translanguaging is most powerful when the teacher knows why a language resource is being opened and what students must ultimately demonstrate.

01

What is the objective?

Identify the actual knowledge or skill students must demonstrate.

02

Where is the barrier?

Is the difficulty conceptual, linguistic, textual, procedural, or combined?

03

Where can flexibility help?

Choose when learners may brainstorm, discuss, annotate, clarify, or rehearse.

04

What remains accountable?

Keep the final product and success criteria aligned with the learning objective.

Guiding question: “What is the language doing for learning at this moment?”
05 / Classroom toolkit

Five moves teachers can use tomorrow.

01 — THINK FILIPINO → WRITE ENGLISH

Reason or jot initial ideas in Filipino, then transform the thinking into an English response.

Best for: writing, reflection, analysis.

02 — BILINGUAL BRAINSTORMING

Generate ideas using any familiar language before organizing the strongest ideas in English.

Best for: prewriting, problem-solving.

03 — MULTILINGUAL VOCABULARY WALL

Display key terms with learner-generated equivalents, explanations, examples, and visuals.

Best for: academic vocabulary.

04 — PEER DISCUSSION

Allow flexible language use during complex reasoning, followed by a target-language synthesis.

Best for: interpretation, debate.

05 — READING SUPPORT

Use bilingual annotation, paraphrasing, glossaries, and familiar-language discussion.

Best for: difficult texts.

06 / Myth-busting

Let's retire five misconceptions.

“English only means better English.”

Not necessarily. Access to prior knowledge can support stronger target-language performance.

“It means students are lazy.”

Strategic linguistic flexibility can support cognitive access while maintaining rigorous outcomes.

“It is just code-switching.”

The pedagogical lens focuses on the learner's full repertoire for meaning-making.

“Teachers must speak every language.”

Peer collaboration and multilingual resources can distribute linguistic expertise.

“It lowers standards.” A scaffold changes the route to learning—not necessarily the learning target.
07 / Lesson lab

The Tell-Tale Heart — but make it multilingual.

Use translanguaging to support literary analysis without lowering the cognitive demand.

Target

Analyze the narrator's thoughts, motivations, and behavior.

Students must: identify textual evidence, interpret the narrator's mental state, explain their reasoning, and construct an English analytical response.

Challenge: How does the narrator reveal an unreliable mind?
Language scaffold

Think in the language that unlocks the idea.

Students may reason, discuss, annotate, and clarify using Filipino or another familiar language.

But: the final analytical claim and evidence-based explanation are produced in English.

Arthur Rackham, Poe's Tales of Mystery and Imagination — public-domain illustration
08 / Lesson sequence

One activity. Four deliberate moves.

1READ

Annotate a selected passage. Mark details revealing the narrator's mental state.

2THINK

Discuss: “Ano ba talaga ang iniisip ng narrator?” Use the language that supports reasoning.

3BRIDGE

Convert the strongest interpretation into an English claim supported by textual evidence.

4SHARE

Present the claim. Classmates challenge or extend the interpretation using evidence.

Key principle: Translanguaging is the scaffold. Literary analysis is the learning objective.
